**Version** 1.0 — 2026-09-16
**Purpose:** The operating manual for the Baby Boomer Business Owner (BBBO) who is systematising
their business to sell at a high multiple. This document IS the dogfood — it proves Kira runs the
business without the owner, which is exactly what a buyer's due diligence requires.

**Read this if:** you are the owner, an operator, a bookkeeper, or a prospective buyer. You do not
need to understand code, architecture, or AI to follow this document. Every concept is explained
through a story about a business you would recognise.

## Preamble

You built your business over 18 years. You know every customer, every supplier, every quirk. But
when you sell it, the buyer is not buying your knowledge — they are buying a system that works
without you. If the business stops when you go on holiday, it is worth less. If it runs the same
(or better) in your absence, it is worth more.

Kira is the system that makes that real. She watches your business, notices what is due, drafts what
to say, applies your approval rules, and only acts after your gate clears. She does not replace your
judgement — she replaces the memory, the timing, and the follow-through that currently live in your
head and your spreadsheet.

This document shows you exactly how she does it, across every part of your business, through
realistic scenarios you can visualise. No code. No jargon. Just stories about how a building products
business runs when the owner is on the golf course.

## The Three Roles (and the One Thing Only You Can Do)

Before we walk through the categories, you need to understand the three roles Kira plays — and the
one role she never takes.

### The Mechanical Clerk

**Analogy:** The accounts clerk who checks the aging report every Monday morning, flags anything
over 30 days, and chases it with the same template email she always uses.

**What she does:** Checks a list, compares it to a rule, and acts. No judgement required. If the
invoice is 30 days overdue, she chases. If the stock is below minimum, she reorders. If the
certificate is expiring, she flags it.

**How she works:** She runs on a schedule (hourly, daily, weekly). She never asks you a question.
She never sends anything without a rule that tells her to. She is deterministic — the same input
always produces the same output.

**When you see her:** You do not. She runs in the background. Her evidence appears in the system
as completed tasks and genome records. You can review her work, but you never need to approve it.

### The Conversational Advisor

**Analogy:** The office manager who hears you say "send a follow-up to that client who went quiet"
and drafts the email, then waits for you to say "send it."

**What she does:** Listens to what you say, classifies your intent, drafts a response, and holds it
for your approval. She is the bridge between your spoken intent and the outside world.

**How she works:** You speak (or type). She classifies: "Is this a quote? An email? A reminder?"
Then she drafts it using your business's voice, your pricing, your history. She shows it to you
and waits. You approve. She sends.

**When you see her:** Every time you give her a task. She is the one you talk to. She never acts
without your approval on the draft.

### The Agentic Specialist

**Analogy:** The senior estimator who takes a complex quote, reads the specs, checks the material
costs, looks up what you charged last time, factors in the crew availability, and builds a
three-page proposal — all without asking you a single question until the end.

**What she does:** Thinks through a multi-step problem. Reads your data. Plans an approach. Drafts
the output. Only asks you when she genuinely needs your decision (not your approval — your
*decision*).

**How she works:** She runs in the background (async). She reads your past pricing, your material
costs, your crew schedule. She plans the approach, drafts the output, and stages it for review.
She costs money per thought (she uses an AI model), so she has a budget cap.

**When you see her:** When she is done. She notifies you that a complex task is ready for review.
You check her work. You approve or reject. She learns from the outcome.

### The One Thing Only You Can Do

**Approve. Decide. Escalate.**

Kira does three things automatically (the Clerk, the Advisor, the Specialist). She never does the
fourth: make a judgement call that changes the direction of the business. That is yours.

When the delegation policy says "hold for approval" — that is you. When the system does not know
what to do — that is you. When a customer calls and wants a deal you have never offered before —
that is you.

Kira remembers your decisions. She builds a pattern. Over time, some decisions become rules, and
the rules replace the approval. But the first time? That is always you.

## Category 1: Finance

**The scenario:** It is the first business day of the month. Your bookkeeper would normally spend
three hours reconciling last month's bank transactions against Xero, coding each one to the right
account, and flagging anything that does not match.

**What the system does:**

The system pulls your bank feed from Xero (the same feed your bookkeeper sees). It matches each
transaction against the corresponding Xero entry — amount, date, reference. It codes unmatched
transactions using the patterns it has learned from your history (every time you coded "Bunnings"
to "Materials — Consumables", the system remembered). It flags anything it cannot match: a
transaction over $5,000 with no reference, a duplicate entry, a payment to an unknown account.

It produces a reconciliation report. It codes the transactions. It flags the exceptions. It does
not send anything, change anything, or close anything. It prepares the work for your bookkeeper to
review.

**The analogy:** This is like a junior accounts clerk who has been coding transactions for three
years and knows your chart of accounts by heart. She does the grunt work. Your bookkeeper reviews
the exceptions and signs off.

**What the owner does:** Nothing, unless the system flags an exception. If a $12,000 payment has
no reference and no matching invoice, the system holds it and asks you: "Do you know what this
is?" You say yes (it is the deposit for the new forklift) or no (investigate it).

**The evidence:** The system records that the finance reconciliation SOP exists, is followed, and
produces clean results. A buyer reviewing the genome sees: "Finance reconciliation runs monthly,
exceptions are flagged and resolved, the SOP has been followed for 12 consecutive months." That
is systematisation.

## Category 2: Accounts Receivable

**The scenario:** A trade customer — let's call them Mitchell & Sons Plumbing — owes you $18,400
on an invoice that is now 60 days overdue. Your bookkeeper has been meaning to chase it but has
been busy with end-of-year.

**What the system does:**

The system watches your Xero aging report. When an invoice crosses 30 days, it sends a polite
follow-up (the "chase" template). When it crosses 60 days, it sends a firmer follow-up with a
payment plan offer. When it crosses 90 days, it escalates to you with a recommendation: "This
customer has not responded to two follow-ups. Do you want to: (a) send a final demand, (b) offer
a payment plan, (c) pass it to collections, (d) write it off?"

The system does not decide which option you pick. It presents the options, attaches the history
(what was sent, when, whether it was opened), and waits for your decision.

**The analogy:** This is like a collections clerk who checks the aging report every Monday, sends
the follow-ups on schedule, and brings you the ones that need your judgement. She does not pick
up the phone and negotiate — she prepares the case and puts it on your desk.

**What the owner does:** Approve the escalation at 90 days. Decide whether to negotiate, collect,
or write off. The system handles everything before that point.

**The evidence:** The system records that the AR follow-up SOP exists and is followed. It records
how many invoices were chased, how many were paid, how many were escalated. A buyer sees: "AR
follow-up runs automatically, escalation happens at defined thresholds, the SOP has been followed
for 12 consecutive months." That is systematisation.

## Category 3: Accounts Payable

**The scenario:** A supplier bill arrives — $12,000 for treated pine from your regular supplier.
The bill needs to be checked (does it match the purchase order?), coded (which job does this go
to?), and approved (is it within the delegation threshold?).

**What the system does:**

The system receives the bill (from email, from Xero, or from a supplier portal). It matches it
against the purchase order (same amount? same quantity? same item?). It codes it to the job
(using the patterns it has learned: "treated pine" goes to "Materials — Timber" on the relevant
job). It checks the delegation threshold: if the bill is under $5,000, it is auto-approved. If it
is over $5,000, it holds for your approval.

The system does not pay the bill. It does not change the supplier terms. It codes, matches, and
routes for approval.

**The analogy:** This is like an accounts payable clerk who checks every invoice against the PO,
codes it to the right job, and brings the ones over the threshold to your desk for a signature.
She does not sign the cheque — she prepares the paperwork.

**What the owner does:** Approve bills above the delegation threshold. Decide if the bill does not
match the PO (dispute it? accept it?).

**The evidence:** The system records that the AP coding SOP exists and is followed. It records
how many bills were matched, how many were exceptions, how many were approved. A buyer sees: "AP
coding runs automatically, exceptions are flagged and resolved, the SOP has been followed for 12
consecutive months." That is systematisation.

## Category 4: Project Management

**The scenario:** A repeat client — Coastal Construction — wants a quote for a $45,000 bathroom
renovation. They have used you twice before. They want the same quality, similar pricing, and
they need the quote by Friday.

**What the system does:**

The system looks up Coastal Construction's history: two previous jobs, both bathrooms, both in the
$35,000–$50,000 range, both at 38% margin. It looks up the current material costs (treated pine
is up 8% since the last quote). It checks crew availability (your best crew has a gap in two
weeks). It drafts the quote using your standard template, with Coastal's pricing history as a
baseline, adjusted for current costs.

It holds the draft for your approval. You review it. You adjust the margin (38% is too tight for
this one — make it 42%). You approve. The system sends the quote.

**The analogy:** This is like your senior estimator who has done a hundred bathroom quotes. He
looks up the last one, adjusts for current costs, checks the schedule, and drafts the proposal.
He brings it to you for a final check before it goes out. He does not send it without your
approval.

**What the owner does:** Approve the quote. Adjust the margin if needed. Decide whether to offer
a discount for the repeat client.

**The evidence:** The system records that the quoting SOP exists and is followed. It records how
long the quote took (from request to sent), how often it was accepted, and what the average margin
was. A buyer sees: "Quoting runs through a standardised process, quotes are drafted automatically
using historical data, the SOP has been followed for 12 consecutive months." That is
systematisation.

## Category 5: Human Resources

**The scenario:** You have hired a new apprentice — Jake, 19, starting Monday. He needs a staff
account, equipment, induction, and a roster spot. Your office manager usually handles this, but she
is on leave.

**What the system does:**

The system runs the onboarding checklist: create the staff record, set up the email account, assign
equipment (hi-vis, tools, phone), schedule the induction (Day 1: safety, Day 2: systems, Day 3:
crew), add him to the roster. It does each step mechanically — there is no judgement in creating
an email account, just a process.

It does not write the employment contract (that is a human task). It does not hold the induction
conversation (that is a human task). It does everything *around* those human tasks: the accounts,
the equipment, the scheduling.

**The analogy:** This is like an HR coordinator who runs through the onboarding checklist while
your office manager is away. She sets up the accounts, orders the equipment, and schedules the
induction. She does not write the contract or hold the conversation — those need a human.

**What the owner does:** Nothing for the mechanical setup. Approve the employment contract (that is
a human task). Hold the induction conversation (that is a human task).

**The evidence:** The system records that the onboarding SOP exists and is followed. It records
how many steps were completed, how long the onboarding took, and whether anything was missed. A
buyer sees: "Onboarding runs through a standardised checklist, the SOP has been followed for every
starter, the system handles the mechanical work while humans handle the judgement." That is
systematisation.

## Category 6: Procurement

**The scenario:** Your treated pine stock has hit the minimum level. You have 12 packs left, and
the minimum is 15. You need to reorder, but your warehouse manager is busy with a delivery.

**What the system does:**

The system watches your stock levels (from Xero or your inventory system). When treated pine hits
the minimum, it drafts a purchase order using the standing terms with your supplier (same quantity,
same price, same delivery window). It holds the PO for your approval (because procurement is above
the delegation threshold).

You approve. The system sends the PO. The supplier confirms. The system records the expected
delivery date and alerts you if it does not arrive on time.

**The analogy:** This is like a warehouse manager who checks stock every morning and places standing
orders when levels drop. He does not decide whether to reorder — the minimum is the rule. He
drafts the PO and brings it to you for a signature.

**What the owner does:** Approve the PO (because procurement is above the delegation threshold).
Decide if the supplier has changed terms or if the price has moved.

**The evidence:** The system records that the procurement SOP exists and is followed. It records
how many reorders were triggered, how many were approved, and whether the stock level stayed above
minimum after each reorder. A buyer sees: "Procurement runs on minimum levels, POs are drafted
automatically, the SOP has been followed for 12 consecutive months." That is systematisation.

## Category 7: Pipeline

**The scenario:** You quoted a job for a developer client 30 days ago — $62,000 for a multi-unit
fit-out. The client said "we will get back to you" and went quiet. You have been meaning to follow
up but have been flat out.

**What the system does:**

The system watches your pipeline. When a quote goes 3 days without a response, it sends a check-in
("Just making sure you got the quote — any questions?"). At 7 days, it sends a value-add ("Here is
a case study of a similar job we completed last month"). At 14 days, it sends a direct ask ("Are
you still considering this? Happy to adjust if the scope has changed"). At 30 days, it escalates
to you: "This lead has gone quiet after three follow-ups. Do you want to: (a) call them personally,
(b) offer a discount, (c) close the lead?"

The system does not decide which option you pick. It presents the options, attaches the history,
and waits.

**The analogy:** This is like a sales coordinator who follows up at day 3, 7, 14, and 30. She
sends the emails, tracks whether they were opened, and brings you the ones that need your personal
attention. She does not call the client herself — she prepares the case and puts it on your desk.

**What the owner does:** Approve the follow-up emails (because pipeline is above the delegation
threshold). Decide at 30 days: call, discount, or close.

**The evidence:** The system records that the pipeline follow-up SOP exists and is followed. It
records how many leads were followed up, how many converted, and how long the average pipeline
cycle was. A buyer sees: "Pipeline follow-up runs automatically, escalation happens at defined
thresholds, the SOP has been followed for 12 consecutive months." That is systematisation.

## Category 8: Compliance

**The scenario:** One of your subcontractors — Dave's Electrical — has an insurance certificate
that expires in 14 days. If Dave works on your site without valid insurance, you are exposed.

**What the system does:**

The system watches your subcontractor register. When a certificate expires in 30 days, it sends a
reminder to the subcontractor ("Your insurance expires on [date]. Please send the renewed
certificate."). At 14 days, it sends a firmer follow-up. At 7 days, it flags the task as urgent
and notifies you: "Dave's Electrical insurance expires in 7 days. If not renewed, we cannot allow
them on site."

The system does not decide whether to let Dave on site. It flags the risk and waits for your
decision.

**The analogy:** This is like a compliance officer who checks the subcontractor register every
week and flags anything expiring. She sends the reminders, tracks the responses, and brings you
the ones that need your decision. She does not decide whether to let someone on site — that is
your call.

**What the owner does:** Nothing for the reminders (fully automated). Decide at 7 days: chase
harder, suspend the subcontractor, or accept the risk.

**The evidence:** The system records that the compliance SOP exists and is followed. It records
how many certificates were checked, how many were expiring, and how many were renewed on time. A
buyer sees: "Compliance monitoring runs automatically, escalation happens at defined thresholds,
the SOP has been followed for 12 consecutive months." That is systematisation.

It holds the message for your approval. You review it. You personalise it (add a note about the

brings you the ones that need your personal touch. She does not call the client herself — she

**What the owner does:** Approve the outreach message. Decide whether to personalise it. Decide
whether to offer a discount to win them back.

**The evidence:** The system records that the BD reconnection SOP exists and is followed. It

## The Escalation Framework (What Happens When the System Does Not Know)

Not every flow is built yet. Of the 140 flows in the task registry, about 10 are fully automated
today. The rest fall into three categories:

### Built and running (Tier M and Tier C)

These are the flows covered in the categories above. The system handles them automatically or with
your approval. The evidence trail is clear.

### Designed but not yet built (Tier A and Tier H)

These are flows the system knows about but has not yet automated. They include:

- **Complex negotiation** (handling a "can you sharpen the price" conversation)
- **Roster optimisation** (building a crew schedule that balances skill, availability, and cost)
- **Incident investigation** (figuring out why a job went over budget)
- **Performance conversations** (having a difficult chat with an underperformer)

The system knows these flows exist. It has the data to support them. But the judgement required is
too complex for the current automation tier. When the system is ready, these flows will be added.

### Not yet designed (the remaining 130 flows)

These are flows that exist in the real business but have not been mapped into the system yet. They
include things like:

- Responding to an inbound call and working out what the person wants
- Handling a social media enquiry
- Writing and posting a job ad
- Running payroll
- Approving a capital purchase

The system does not pretend to handle these. When a new flow is needed, the process is:

1. **Map the flow** — describe what happens, who does it, what the trigger is
2. **Classify the tier** — is it mechanical (Clerk), conversational (Advisor), or agentic (Specialist)?
3. **Build the rule or agent** — add the flow to the system
4. **Test it** — run it against real data, verify the output
5. **Deploy it** — add it to the live system

This is how the system grows: one flow at a time, each one proven before it runs.

## How to Read This as a Buyer

If you are reviewing this document as a prospective buyer, here is what you should look for:

### The evidence trail

Every category above produces evidence. The system records:

- **What was done** — every task, every follow-up, every approval
- **When it was done** — timestamps, intervals, response times
- **What the outcome was** — paid, escalated, approved, closed
- **What the system learned** — patterns, thresholds, delegation rules

This evidence is stored in the **genome** — the business's memory of how it operates. A buyer can
review the genome and see: "This business has been running the same SOPs for 12 consecutive months.
The owner is not in the loop for 80% of the work. The system handles the mechanical work, the
advisory work, and the complex work — and the owner only gets involved for the judgement calls."

### The "runs without the owner" proof

The continuity dashboard shows:

- **How many tasks ran without the owner** — the mechanical Clerk handled them
- **How many tasks needed the owner's approval** — the Conversational Advisor drafted them
- **How many tasks needed the owner's decision** — the Agentic Specialist planned them

A buyer wants to see: most tasks run without the owner. The owner is involved only for the
judgement calls that genuinely require human expertise. The system handles the rest.

### The systematisation evidence

The genome records:

- **Every SOP that exists** — finance, AR, AP, PM, HR, procurement, pipeline, compliance, BD
- **Every SOP that is followed** — the system has run it consistently, not just documented it
- **Every SOP that produces results** — the evidence shows the outcomes, not just the activity

A buyer wants to see: the business is not just documented — it is *operationalised*. The SOPs are
not sitting in a folder. They are running, producing evidence, and improving over time.
